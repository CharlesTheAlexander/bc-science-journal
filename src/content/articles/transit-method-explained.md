Hold a marble in front of a floodlight from across a football field. You will not see the marble. But if you had a light meter good enough, you would notice the floodlight get dimmer by a tiny, repeatable amount every time the marble crossed it. That is the transit method, and it is how the Kepler space telescope found more than 2,600 confirmed planets around other stars.

## The dip

When a planet passes between us and its star, it blocks a fraction of the star's disk. The fraction is just the ratio of the two areas. Jupiter's radius is about a tenth of the Sun's, so Jupiter transiting the Sun would block about one percent of its light. Earth, with a radius about a hundredth of the Sun's, would block one part in ten thousand. Detecting an Earth-sized planet means measuring a star's brightness to better than 0.01 percent, and doing it for hours at a time, from a platform that does not have an atmosphere twinkling in front of it. That is why Kepler and its successor TESS are in space.

![A light curve: a flat line with a small, flat-bottomed dip](../../images/covers/transit-method-explained.svg)
*A transit light curve. The depth of the dip gives the planet's size; the spacing between dips gives its year.*

The shape of the dip carries information. Its depth gives the planet's size relative to the star. Its duration, combined with the star's size, constrains the planet's orbital speed and therefore its distance. And its repetition is the clock: if the dip comes back every 365 days, the planet's year is 365 days. Astronomers usually require at least three transits before they call something a candidate, because one dip could be anything and two could be a coincidence.

## What a transit does not tell you

The transit method has a built-in bias. A planet only transits if its orbit is lined up almost edge-on with our line of sight. For a planet at Earth's distance from a Sun-like star, that alignment happens for roughly one star in two hundred. So every transiting planet stands in for hundreds we cannot see this way, and the statistics of how many planets exist are worked out by correcting for that geometry.

The transit also gives size but not mass, and without mass you cannot tell whether a planet is rock or gas. For mass, astronomers turn to a second method: the star's wobble. A planet's gravity tugs its star in a small circle, which shifts the star's light slightly bluer as it approaches and redder as it recedes. Measuring that shift gives the planet's mass. Size from the transit plus mass from the wobble gives density, and density is what tells you whether you have found another Neptune or another Earth.

> One star in two hundred is lined up right. Every transit we see stands in for the hundreds we cannot.

## What comes next

Transits now do more than count planets. When a planet crosses its star, a sliver of starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere on the way to us. Molecules in that atmosphere absorb specific wavelengths, so the transit looks very slightly deeper at those wavelengths. The James Webb Space Telescope has used this to detect water vapor, carbon dioxide, and methane in the atmospheres of planets tens of light-years away.

That is the strange thing about the method. It began as a way to notice a shadow. It has become a way to read the chemistry of a world nobody will ever visit, one dimming at a time.

## Trying it yourself

The transit method is one of the few discovery techniques in astronomy that amateurs really contribute to. Backyard telescopes with a decent camera can detect the transits of large planets around bright stars, because a Jupiter-sized dip of one percent is within reach of careful photometry from the ground. Groups of amateur observers regularly refine the timing of known transits, which helps professional telescopes plan their observations. The data from Kepler and TESS is also public, and there are citizen-science projects where volunteers look at light curves that automated searches skipped. Several planets have been found that way, by people who noticed a dip a computer did not.
