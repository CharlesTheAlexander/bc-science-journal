The course catalog gives AP Biology two sentences. This is the longer version, written the week after the exam, for anyone deciding whether to take it. It is one student's experience, and your teacher and your year will differ.

## What the year covers

The College Board organizes the course into eight units, and most teachers follow them in order: chemistry of life, cell structure, cellular energetics, cell communication and the cell cycle, heredity, gene expression and regulation, natural selection, and ecology. The first half of the year is molecular and small. The second half zooms out to populations and ecosystems. Many people find one half much more interesting than the other; almost nobody likes both equally.

The pace is the thing to know. In a regular biology course, you spend a week on a topic. In AP, you spend two or three days. The reading is roughly a textbook chapter a week, and it is the kind of reading you have to do before class rather than after, because class time assumes you have seen the vocabulary already.

## The labs

The course is built around thirteen lab investigations, and the labs are the best part. You will run gel electrophoresis, transform bacteria with a plasmid so they glow, measure photosynthesis in leaf disks, and model evolution with actual data. The labs are also where the writing happens. Each one ends with a report, and the reports get graded on whether your conclusion follows from your data, not on whether you got the "right" result.

- Expect one lab report every two or three weeks.
- Expect lab work to spill into a second class period. Bacteria do not care about the bell.
- Expect at least one lab to fail. The write-up of a failed lab is often the most instructive.

## The exam

The AP exam is three hours: sixty multiple-choice questions in ninety minutes, then six free-response questions in ninety minutes. The free-response section is where the course's emphasis on data shows up. Most questions hand you a graph or a table from a real experiment and ask you to interpret it, design a follow-up, or predict what a change would do. Memorization gets you through the multiple choice. Reasoning from data gets you through the free response.

> The exam is not asking whether you know biology. It is asking whether you can think like someone who does.

## Who likes it

Students who liked the experiments in regular biology more than the vocabulary tend to like AP Biology. Students who want a science course with less math than AP Chemistry or AP Physics tend to like it, though there is statistics in the ecology and genetics units. Students who are taking it because it "looks good" tend to find the reading load punishing by November.

## What I would do differently

Start the reading habit in September. The students who fell behind fell behind on reading, not on understanding. And keep a running list of every graph you interpret in class, because the exam is made of those. By May, that list is the only review you need.

## What it leads to

AP Biology is the course most students take before a summer research program in the life sciences, and it is the one that makes those applications credible. It also connects to the school's other science offerings in ways the catalog does not spell out: the statistics you use in the ecology unit is the same statistics you need to write up an independent project, and the lab report format is the format this journal uses for research pieces. If you are thinking about writing for the journal, the lab reports you produce in this course are drafts of what could be published, and several of the pieces in this issue started that way. Take it if you want to do science rather than read about it. Skip it if you mainly want the acronym on a transcript; there are easier ways to get one.

One practical note: the course is a full year and the exam is in early May, which means the last three weeks of class are review and the last unit gets compressed. Plan for ecology to arrive fast.
