*Sample Alum '09 graduated from Berkeley Carroll, studied chemistry in college, and completed a doctorate in materials science. They now work in an industrial research lab. This interview has been edited for length and clarity.*

**When did you first think science was something you could do for a living, rather than just a class?**

Junior year, honestly. I had a chemistry teacher who ran a lab where the answer was not in the back of the book, and I remember the specific feeling of not knowing what the result would be. Up to then, every lab was a demonstration of something already known. That one was small, it was about reaction rates, but the not-knowing was the hook.

**What did you do in college that mattered most?**

Getting into a research lab early. I emailed six professors sophomore year and one said yes. I washed glassware for a semester and then got a tiny piece of a real project. Everything after that, the graduate school applications, the letters, the sense of what research actually is, came from that one yes. The advice I give everyone is to send the emails and expect five silences.

**What is a PhD actually like?**

Long, and mostly not what you picture. The picture is discovery. The reality is that most weeks are troubleshooting: an instrument is down, a synthesis did not work, a result will not replicate. About once a year something works and it is the best feeling in the world, and that is what carries the other fifty-one weeks. The median time to a doctorate in the physical sciences is a little under six years. Nobody says that out loud at the recruiting weekend.

> About once a year something works, and that carries the other fifty-one weeks.

**Was there something you wish you had known in high school?**

Two things. First, that being good at the math is necessary but not the thing that makes a good scientist. The thing is tolerating being wrong for a long time without quitting. Second, that writing matters far more than I expected. I spend more of my week writing than doing experiments: proposals, reports, papers, emails explaining results to people who were not in the room. A journal like this one is honestly good practice for that.

**What do you do now, day to day?**

I work on battery materials. A typical day is a morning of reading, a few hours at an instrument or in a glovebox, and an afternoon of data and meetings. Industry is faster than academia and more constrained. You work on what the company needs, but you also see your work turn into a product, which I like.

**Advice for a student reading this who is interested but not sure?**

Do one real project. Not a class assignment, a thing where you do not know the answer. It can be small. Then write about it, because the writing is where you find out whether you understood what you did. If you liked both halves, the doing and the explaining, you will probably like research.


**One last question. What would you have written for a journal like this in high school?**

Probably the reaction-rate lab, honestly. It was small and it did not entirely work, and I think that is the kind of thing worth writing up. Most of what gets published in science is the stuff that worked. The stuff that did not is where you actually learn to do it. If a student here has a project that half-worked and they can explain which half and why, that is a better piece than a polished summary of something in a textbook, and it is a better thing to be able to show someone later.


**Any regrets about the path?**

Only the ordinary ones. I wish I had taken more statistics earlier, because every field I have touched turned out to run on it. And I wish I had known that changing direction is normal: I started in one subfield and finished in another, and nobody held it against me. Students sometimes think the first choice is binding. It is a first draft.
