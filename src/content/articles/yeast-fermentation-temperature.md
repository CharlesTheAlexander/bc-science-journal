Every bread recipe says roughly the same thing: dissolve the yeast in warm water, not hot. We wanted to know what "warm" actually means to the yeast, and whether the curve of activity against temperature looks like the smooth hump that textbooks draw for enzymes. This is a write-up of a small independent project done over three weekends. It is not a controlled laboratory study, and we say below where it fell short.

## What we did

Baker's yeast, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, breaks down sugar and releases carbon dioxide and ethanol when it does not have enough oxygen. The gas is easy to catch. We put 1 gram of dry yeast and 5 grams of table sugar into 100 milliliters of water in a plastic bottle, stretched a balloon over the neck, and measured the balloon's circumference with a tape every five minutes for forty minutes.

We ran this at five water-bath temperatures: 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 degrees Celsius. The baths were a mixing bowl of water with ice or hot tap water added, and a cooking thermometer to check. We did three bottles at each temperature, fifteen bottles total, across three separate days.

Circumference is a rough stand-in for gas volume. A balloon is not a linear container: it resists stretching more at first, then less, then more again. We treated the change in circumference over the first thirty minutes as our rate and compared temperatures against each other, rather than trying to convert to milliliters.

## What we found

- At 10 degrees, the balloons barely moved in forty minutes. Two of the three never inflated past limp.
- At 20 degrees, all three inflated slowly and steadily.
- At 30 degrees, inflation was clearly faster, roughly twice the 20-degree rate.
- At 40 degrees, the rate was the highest we saw, but the three bottles disagreed with each other more than at any other temperature.
- At 50 degrees, inflation started fast and then stalled. By forty minutes two bottles had stopped growing entirely.

The rough doubling between 20 and 30 degrees matches a rule of thumb biologists call the Q10, which says that many biological reaction rates roughly double for every 10-degree rise until something breaks. The stall at 50 degrees is probably that something breaking. Yeast cells begin to die in the mid-50s Celsius, and our water baths were not stable enough to be sure we stayed below that line.

> The 40-degree bottles gave us the fastest rate and the least agreement, which is the kind of result that tells you your method is at its limit.

## What we are unsure about

We are fairly confident in the shape of the curve: slow when cold, fastest somewhere between 30 and 40 degrees, collapsing above that. We are not confident about where the peak is. The 40-degree bottles varied so much that we cannot say whether 35 or 45 would have been faster, and our baths drifted by several degrees over forty minutes, especially the hot ones.

Three things would make this a real experiment. A water bath that holds temperature, so the number we report is the number the yeast experienced. A gas measurement that is actually linear, like displacing water in an inverted graduated cylinder. And more than three replicates at each temperature, since the spread at 40 degrees was larger than the difference between neighboring temperatures.

What we can say is that the recipe advice holds. Warm means about body temperature. Hot tap water, which can come out above 50 degrees, is not a small mistake for the yeast. It is the end of the experiment.

## Why this is worth doing badly first

A version of this experiment appears in many biology curricula, usually with the temperatures chosen in advance and the result known. Doing it without the answer key changed what we noticed. The textbook curve is smooth; ours was jagged, and the jaggedness came from the equipment, not the yeast. Learning to tell those two apart, the signal from the setup, was more useful than the curve itself. If you try this, keep the balloons and the bottles, but spend your effort on the water baths. Everything else follows from holding the temperature still.
