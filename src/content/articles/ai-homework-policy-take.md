Every school we know of has now written an AI policy, and most of them read like the same document. Do not submit work generated by a model. Cite it if you used it. Detectors may be used. The policies are reasonable, and they are also beside the point, because they treat a language model as a new way to cheat rather than as a change in what a piece of homework can prove.

## What an assignment used to prove

A five-paragraph essay on the causes of the French Revolution was never really about the French Revolution. It was a proxy. If you turned in a coherent essay, a teacher could infer that you had read something, organized it, and written sentences in order. The essay was evidence of a process.

A language model breaks the proxy. It can produce the essay without the process. The essay still exists; the evidence is gone. That is a different problem from cheating, and it is not solved by detection. Detectors are unreliable, they flag non-native English writers at higher rates, and a student who lightly rewrites model output beats them anyway.

## Two bad answers

The first bad answer is a ban. It has the advantage of clarity and the disadvantage of being unenforceable and slightly dishonest. Students will use these tools in college and at work, and pretending they do not exist teaches nothing about using them well. It also turns every assignment into a suspicion.

The second bad answer is to allow everything and hope. This produces students who can prompt and cannot write. Writing is not the typing part. It is the part where you discover you did not understand something until you tried to say it. Outsourcing that outsources the thinking.

> The essay still exists. The evidence it used to carry is gone.

## The better question

The question worth asking about any assignment is: which part of this is the learning, and can a model do that part? Then design the assignment so the learning part is the part a student has to do.

- If the point is knowing the material, assess it in a room, on paper or in conversation. This is old technology and it works.
- If the point is the writing, ask for drafts, notes, and revisions, and grade the change between them. A model can write a draft; it cannot show how your second draft answered your teacher's comment on the first.
- If the point is research, ask for the sources and what they say, not just the conclusion. Models still invent citations, and checking a source is a skill worth having anyway.
- If a model would genuinely help, say so, and ask students to include what they asked it and what they changed. That is closer to how professionals will use these tools than either secrecy or a ban.

## What this journal decided

We wrote our own rule before our first issue, and we tried to follow our own advice. Writers may use AI to brainstorm and to check grammar. The writing and the reasoning must be theirs, editors read with that in mind, and every factual claim needs a source a human checked. We do not run detectors. We do run edits, and an edit round exposes a hollow piece faster than any classifier.

That is not a perfect policy. It is an honest one, and it is the kind of policy schools should be writing: one that says what the work is for, rather than one that only says what is forbidden.

## A note on humility

We are students writing about how schools should treat students, and we are aware of the bias. Teachers are the ones who have to read forty essays and decide which ones a person wrote. Our proposal asks more of them, not less: drafts take longer to grade than final copies, and conversations take longer than tests. That cost is real. What we are claiming is that the alternative, a policy built entirely around catching people, costs more in a way that does not show up on a rubric. It costs the assumption of good faith, and a classroom without that assumption is a worse place to learn, with or without a model in it.
